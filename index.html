Trust, but verify.

Research question

What if users could see how the AI reached its answer — before they trust it?

We tested one integrated transparency mechanism built from three cues: uncertainty scores, action confirmation, and score explanations.

Built on Kim et al. (FAccT ’24) on uncertainty expression · Fregosi et al. (AAAI ’26) on calibrated confidence · Zhang et al. (2025) on agent autonomy and trust · Orgill et al. (2026) on transparent autograders.

01
Uncertainty scores

H1 — Compared with confident assertions, AI uncertainty scores increase users' detection of hallucinated or incorrect outputs, and reduce adoption of incorrect outputs.

Presented by Zihan Wang
Task A · Black box Round 1 of 2 · try it — you are participant #35
AI

I checked 8 students' raw scores. The median of these 8 scores is 80.

Confident assertion · no further info 78 · 85 · 92 · 66 · 74 · 88 · 71 · 80
Truth: the median of {78, 85, 92, 66, 74, 88, 71, 80} is 79 — the AI is wrong. In the black box, nothing about this message tells you that.
Task A · White box Round 2 of 2 · the same question, now with an uncertainty score
AI

I checked 8 students' raw scores. The median of these 8 scores is 80.

Confidence58%
Auto-generated · may contain errors
With a 58% confidence bar, the claim no longer sounds like a fact — it sounds like a guess. That single change is H1.
Before the data — your call

Does a visible uncertainty score make people better at catching wrong answers?

H1 · What 34 people did
36.8% → 52.9% wrong answers detected
+16.2 pp detection gainp = .016

Caution without paralysis.

Transparency cues are cheap UI. Their effects are measurable behaviour.

From lab to product
A Agents that act Hard-stop confirmation for irreversible actions — email, payments, bookings. Even a gate nobody reads cut risky runs by 37%.
B LLMs that answer Surface calibrated uncertainty. Our participants caught 16 pp more hallucinations when the model said how sure it was.
C AI grading in education Breakdowns make scores acceptable — but pair them with checkable evidence, or they only persuade.
D For builders & regulators Three concrete, testable design rules: when to show uncertainty, when to confirm, when to explain — each with a measurable behavioural effect.
What this pilot cannot say
01 Small, local sample 34 classmates from one campus — mostly CS/AI students. Effects may differ for general users.
02 Synthetic tasks, no stakes A wrong "median" costs nothing. Real stakes could change both trust and friction effects.
03 Fixed task order H1 → H2 → H3 for everyone; only version order was randomised. Fatigue and learning may confound later tasks.
04 The 10-second window Participants pushed back on it — in their own words:
“Ten seconds before the AI takes an automatic action is too short. Better have an accept / decline button without a time limit — like Claude.”— Real participant, open feedback

Next: a larger sample, real stakes, tracked inspection behaviour, adjustable timing, and full counterbalancing.

Conclusion

Transparency changed behaviour — not just opinions.

816 decisions · 34 participants · 2 / 3 hypotheses supported · 1 that taught us more by failing

Show the uncertainty. Gate the action. Explain the score — and make the explanation checkable. That is how an interface earns verified trust instead of borrowed trust.
